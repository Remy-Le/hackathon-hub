## 1. I found the perfect hackathon two weeks after it ended

I saw the recap post before I saw the announcement.

A 48-hour climate-tech hackathon, two train stops from where I live. Great sponsors, a prize pool that actually covered a semester of ramen, and — based on the photos — exactly my kind of chaos. Whiteboards covered in half-erased architecture diagrams. Someone asleep under a table at 4am. A demo that clearly broke on stage and got a standing ovation anyway.

I found out about all of it from a LinkedIn recap. Two weeks late.

Here's the thing — it's not like the event was hiding. It was posted somewhere. Probably a university Discord I'm not in, or a Luma page that never made it into my feed, or a WhatsApp group three friends-of-friends removed from mine. Every hackathon lives somewhere. The problem is that "somewhere" is a different place every single time.

So you don't really miss out on hackathons because they're rare. You miss out because discovery in Europe is a lottery — and you only find out you lost after the fact, when the recap post shows up and it's too late to do anything but feel it.

If you've had this exact moment — scrolling past photos of an event you would have killed to attend, wondering how you didn't know it existed — you're not bad at looking. The system's just bad at telling you.

## 2. Two great hackathons, same weekend — why does this keep happening?

At some point you stop being surprised by it. You'll have two genuinely good options — different countries, different themes, both worth a weekend of your life — and they land on the exact same three days.

It's not a coincidence, and it's not just bad luck. Hackathon organizers don't coordinate with each other across borders. A university in Lisbon and a fintech accelerator in Warsaw have zero reason to check each other's calendars before picking a date. Multiply that by the number of hackathons happening across Europe on any given weekend, and collisions aren't the exception — they're the default.

Which means the actual decision you're making isn't "should I go to this hackathon." It's "which one am I going to have to turn down." And you're usually making that call with incomplete information, because you found out about one of them three days ago and the other one an hour ago, from two completely different sources, with no way to see them side by side until it's almost too late to plan travel for either.

The frustrating part isn't the choice — competing options are a good problem to have. It's that you're forced to choose *blind*, one link at a time, instead of seeing the full weekend laid out in front of you before you commit to either one.

## 3. Why "travel reimbursement" is the filter nobody talks about

Everyone talks about prize money. Nobody talks about the thing that actually decides whether a student can show up at all: can I afford to get there.

A hackathon three hours away by train might as well be on another continent if you're a student with no travel budget and rent due in a week. And that's the quiet filter sitting underneath every "which hackathon should I go to" decision — not skill level, not theme, not even interest. Just: does this one help me get there, or am I paying for the privilege of building something for free.

Some events do cover travel. A decent number, actually — sponsors want the room full, so reimbursement budgets exist. But try finding that information *before* you've already fallen in love with an event, built a team around it, and then discovered three days out that travel support was never on the table. It's usually buried in an FAQ, or mentioned once in a Discord announcement, or just... not written down anywhere until you email someone and wait four days for a reply.

So the real question isn't "which hackathons are worth attending." It's "which hackathons are actually *reachable*, on my budget, without gambling a week of anxious waiting on an email reply." And right now, there's no easy way to filter for that up front — you find out you could've gone the same way you find out most things about hackathons in Europe: too late, and by accident.
