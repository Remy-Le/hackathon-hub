## Organizers

### 1. (Unaware → Problem Aware) You don't have a visibility problem. You have a sorting problem.

Every organizer we've talked to describes the same frustration in the same words: "we need more people to know about this." So they post in every Discord, every group chat, every mailing list they can find — and registrations go up. Then 40-50% of those registrants don't show.

Here's the reframe: you were never short on reach. Generic promotion into fifteen channels *works*, in the sense that it produces numbers. What it doesn't produce is the right people — participants who match your theme, your skill bar, your actual audience. No-show rate isn't a marketing failure, it's a targeting failure wearing a marketing costume.

The fix isn't louder. It's more precise. Reaching 500 loosely-relevant people and losing half of them to no-shows is worse than reaching 150 people who actually wanted to be there.

### 2. (Problem Aware) The sponsor grind starts from zero every single year

Ask any organizer who's run more than one edition of a hackathon what the worst part is, and it's rarely the event itself — it's rebuilding the sponsor pipeline from scratch every cycle. Last year's sponsor contacts go cold, new ones have to be cold-emailed, and there's no institutional memory that carries the relationship forward.

That's not a "you" problem, it's structural: nothing connects one organizer's sponsor relationships to the next organizer's, or to a regional network that could actually broker warm intros instead of cold ones.

### 3. (Solution Aware) One submission, pan-European distribution — no MLH sanctioning required

You know MLH exists, and Devpost, and doing it yourself through your own network. MLH works if you're inside its affiliate system; going fully independent means doing all the promotion legwork yourself, every time.

Submit your event to Hackathon Hub once and it's automatically categorized and distributed to a pan-European audience of participants actively filtering for events like yours — no affiliate application, no gatekeeping. You're not trading your independence for reach.

### 4. (Product Aware) The ambassador network is the part that actually moves the needle

The thing that makes this different from "just another listing site" is the 90+ ambassador network actively sourcing participants and sponsors on the ground in 25+ countries — not a passive directory waiting for organizers to find it, but people actively connecting local communities to a bigger one.

Reduce no-shows by reaching hackers with an actual track record via the leaderboard, not just anyone who clicked "register."

## Sponsors / Corporate Recruiters

### 5. (Unaware) The European tech talent pool spends its weekends in rooms you're not in

Every weekend, somewhere in Europe, a room full of students and early-career developers is pulling all-nighters building things under pressure — exactly the conditions that surface who can actually ship. That's the room recruiters want to be in. Most sponsors only ever see a slice of it, whichever hackathons happen to be on their radar that quarter.

### 6. (Problem Aware) 90% of sponsors never meet a future employee on-site

Sponsorship budgets get justified as "brand visibility" because nobody can prove the recruiting ROI. The uncomfortable stat: on the median sponsored hackathon, roughly 90% of sponsors never actually meet someone they go on to hire. The logo goes up, the swag goes out, and the actual hiring signal — who's good, who's reliable, who ships under pressure — gets lost in a room of 200 people you had two hours with.

Add in travel, staff time on-site, and the swag budget, and the real cost of a sponsorship is often double the number on the invoice. For that price, "we were in the room" isn't a return.

### 7. (Solution Aware) A sourcing layer, not a logo wall

You know the options: sponsor directly through MLH, sponsor a big-name event like Junction, or build relationships with organizers yourself. All of them put your name on a banner. None of them guarantee the *right* people notice it.

The ambassador network changes that math — 90+ people across 25+ countries who are actively sourcing and connecting participants and sponsors, not just distributing your logo to a room. It's a targeted layer between "we sponsored an event" and "we actually met candidates worth pursuing."

### 8. (Product Aware) We're Phase 1 and we're not hiding it

No published pricing yet. No case studies yet. That's the honest state of the B2B side of Hackathon Hub right now, and we'd rather tell you that up front than oversell a track record we don't have.

What you get instead: transparency about where we are, and access to a community-verified, ambassador-sourced network that's already covering 615+ events in 33+ countries — the infrastructure is real even if the sales motion is still early.

## Ambassadors

### 9. (Recruiting) Your region shouldn't be an island

If you're already deep in your local hackathon or student-tech community, you've probably felt the ceiling: your network is real, but it stops at your city or your country's border. Meanwhile there's a whole continent of hackathons, sponsors, and other regional organizers doing the exact same work you are, with no bridge between you.

Becoming an ambassador means joining a 90+ person network across 25+ countries actively shaping the European hackathon scene — real recognition, real perks, and a reach that goes further than any one community can build alone.

### 10. (Recruiting, expectation-setting) Phase 1, said plainly

We're not going to oversell what this program is yet. It's early — Hackathon Hub itself is Phase 1, pre-monetization, still building. That means ambassador perks and support are still developing too. What's already real: 48-hour average response time from the team, and a genuinely active network — not a title that sits unused.

If you want to help build something at the ground floor of the European hackathon scene rather than join something fully finished, this is that.
