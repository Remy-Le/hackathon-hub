## Level 1 — Unaware
*Mindset: doesn't know they're missing anything. Goes to hackathons "when a friend mentions one." Sell the missed experience, not the product. No CTA beyond an emotional hook.*

### 1. The 3am version of you that you never got to meet

There's a version of you that exists at 3am on the second night of a hackathon — wired on bad coffee, arguing about a schema with someone you met eleven hours ago, half-convinced the thing you're building is either genius or garbage, no in-between.

That version of you doesn't show up at a desk job. It doesn't show up in a lecture hall. It shows up exactly once, in a room full of strangers who are all doing the same insane thing you are, and it tends to make friends — or cofounders — out of whoever's next to it when the demo breaks at 8am and somehow still gets a laugh.

Most people who've had that night talk about it like it's the realest thing that happened to them all year. And most people who haven't had it yet don't know they're one weekend away from it.

How many of those weekends went by this year without you in the room?

---

### 2. Nobody warns you that "just for fun" hackathons change what you do next

You don't go into a hackathon planning to change your major, quit your internship search, or start a company with someone you just met. You go in because it sounded fun and there was pizza.

But something about 36 hours of building something from nothing, next to people who are just as underslept and overcommitted as you are, has a habit of rearranging what people think they're capable of. The team that built a dumb joke project sophomore year is sometimes the same team that raises a seed round three years later. Ask anyone who's been to a few — the "for fun" ones are rarely just for fun in hindsight.

You don't need to be looking for your cofounder or your next career pivot to want in on that. You just need to be in the room when it happens.

## Level 3 — Solution Aware
*Mindset: knows aggregators exist (Devpost, MLH) but finds them thin on Europe. Position the EU-specific category — "5x more events for Europe."*

### 3. Devpost is built for the whole world. That's exactly the problem.

Devpost is a fine product — if "fine" means "built to cover hackathons on every continent, which means it's built to cover none of them especially well." For a hackathon happening in Lisbon next month, Devpost's coverage of the European scene is a rounding error next to its coverage of the US.

That's not a knock on Devpost. It's just math. A platform trying to be the front door for hackathons everywhere on Earth cannot also be the platform that knows about the 40-person student hackathon in Ljubljana, or the fintech challenge running out of a Warsaw co-working space, or the AI weekend three train stops from you that a university society posted about in a Discord server Devpost will never index.

We only do Europe. That's the entire pitch. Which is why we're currently sitting on 5x more European hackathons than Devpost covers for the *entire planet* — not because we're bigger, but because we're not trying to be everywhere. We're trying to be complete in exactly one place.

If "aggregator" is the category you're already shopping in, the question isn't which one has the most logos. It's which one actually has the event happening near you next month.

---

### 4. MLH is great if you're already inside the network. What if you're not?

Major League Hacking has real infrastructure — sponsor relationships, a hacker rank system, a track record. If you're already plugged into that ecosystem, it works.

But MLH's gravity is mostly toward its own affiliated events, and its footprint outside the US and its partner universities thins out fast. If your hackathon isn't MLH-sanctioned, or you're a student at a university without an MLH chapter, you're outside the system it was built for — and Europe has hundreds of hackathons that were never going to be MLH events in the first place: independent community meetups, corporate innovation challenges, national student competitions with zero MLH affiliation.

We're not trying to replace MLH's network — we're covering the much larger set of European events that never had a network to plug into. Community-submitted, ambassador-verified, and not gated behind any single organization's sanctioning process.

## Level 4 — Product Aware
*Mindset: knows Hackathon Hub exists, hesitates on legitimacy/freshness of a "Phase 1" platform. Lead with proof + reassurance.*

### 5. 615+ hackathons. 33 countries. Zero ads. Here's the actual proof.

You've probably landed on the site once already and thought "okay, but is this actually maintained, or is it another directory that goes stale in six months?" Fair question — there are enough dead hackathon-listing side projects out there to make that skepticism reasonable.

Here's what's actually true right now: 615+ events listed across 33+ countries, 144 live at any given moment, submitted and maintained by a community of organizers and 90+ ambassadors actively working in 25+ countries — not a scraper, not an intern doing a data dump once a quarter. No ads. No tracking. The business model is donations, and we say that outright instead of dodging the question, because "Phase 1, still figuring out monetization" is the honest answer and we'd rather you hear it from us than assume the worst.

The AI search (once you're logged in) understands synonyms and multiple languages, so you're not stuck guessing the exact English keyword an organizer used. Filter by country, city, prize money, travel support, theme, duration — whatever actually matters to your decision.

You don't have to take our word for the coverage. Go look at what's live in your country right now.

---

### 6. The leaderboard nobody else has built

Every hackathon platform before this one measured your standing *inside one organizer's event*. Win a university hackathon, and that win lives on that university's page and nowhere else. There was no way to build a track record that followed you across events, across countries, across organizers.

Hackathon Hub's leaderboard is the first one independent from any single organizer — a cross-event ranking that actually accumulates. Climb it and you're not just bragging rights within one community; top standing has led to invites to elite invite-only events and investor/VC mixers that never would've seen you otherwise.

It's a small thing until it isn't — the same way a GitHub contribution graph is a small thing until a hiring manager pulls it up.

## Level 5 — Most Aware
*Mindset: regular user, knows the brand, just wants the best next match. Simple recall + status. Short-form, notification-style — not full articles.*

### 7. "You're #14 on the leaderboard. Three hackers just passed you."

Short recall notification / push copy — pairs with an in-app or email nudge:

> Someone just took your spot. #14 → #17 this week. Three new events near you closed registration in the last 48 hours — the next one's still open.

### 8. "New this month, near you"

Localized monthly digest opener:

> [X] new hackathons went live in [region] this month. [Y] still have open registration. Filtered to your saved criteria — [prize range / theme / travel support].

### 9. "The one you almost missed"

Re-engagement copy for someone who viewed but didn't register:

> You looked at [Event Name] three days ago. Registration closes [date]. Still time.

*Note: these three are format templates, not full posts — meant to be populated dynamically per-user for email/push/in-app, not published as static content.*
